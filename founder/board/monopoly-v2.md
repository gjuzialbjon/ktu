# Monopoly lens
Score: 4 | Vote: PASS

## What is strong

- **The small-market instinct is right.** The Monopoly lens says start with a big share of a small market, and the wedge — small informal events in one city, explicitly unserved by Instagram/Facebook and the three local platforms — is exactly that shape. Owning every yoga class, book club and hike in Tirana before touching concerts is the correct sequencing.
- **Distribution comes before product.** The lens says a good product with no way to reach buyers is not a business. Stage 1 builds the audience (a complete weekly feed) before a line of code exists, so if the app ever ships it ships into a distribution channel the founders already own. Most pitches get this backwards; this one does not.
- **The end state is the right kind of moat.** A one-city events platform, if it wins, is a two-sided network effect: organizers go where attendees are and vice versa. The founders name this themselves, and it is the only durable advantage on the lens's list that this business could plausibly build. Economies of scale in curation are real but minor; brand is possible later.
- **Capital discipline.** Pre-selling 10 organizers before building, and a hard stop at each gate, means the market is interrogated at near-zero cost. The lens does not award points for cheapness, but it does punish businesses that spend before they know their secret — this plan cannot.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)

1. **Nothing stops a copycat in month six — and the plan broadcasts the invitation.** The lens's sharpest question has the worst possible answer here. Stages 1–3 are an Instagram account, a WhatsApp channel and a spreadsheet: zero proprietary technology, zero accumulated network effect, and the curation know-how is replicable by anyone with free evenings. Worse, a *successful* Stage 1 is a public demand signal — a few thousand followers on a visible feed tells every café owner, agency and bored developer in Tirana that the market exists, before the founders have anything defensible. The brief itself concedes that "a local copycat beats absentee founders," yet two of the three founders are in Barcelona and London, all three are part-time, and the full-time founder is unnamed and unfunded. The plan's own logic says the most dangerous window is exactly the window the plan creates. The network-effect moat only exists at Stage 4–5; everything before it is a free R&D program for whoever moves faster locally.
2. **The wedge may be a market with no profit pool — a monopoly over nothing.** The lens wants a small market you can *dominate and then profitably expand from*. Small informal events are unserved possibly because they are unservable: yoga by the lake and book clubs are free or near-free, so "ticketing commission on money that already flows" collects a cut of money that mostly does not flow in this segment. The paying organizers (venues, promoters, commercial events) are precisely where Kuadr Events, Enter Event and Go Wild already sit — so the expansion step walks out of the monopoly and into the competition the lens says to avoid. Dominating the niche could validate attention while generating no revenue, and the staged gates would read green right up to that wall: Stage 1 measures followers, not payers, and 10 token pre-sales from personally-onboarded organizers is a friends-and-family signal, not a price.
3. **There is no secret, and the unexplained graveyard might hide a negative one.** The lens asks what the founders know that others do not. The fragmentation of Tirana's event listings is visible to everyone — three local platforms already acted on it and stalled, and the brief admits nobody knows why. If the answer is "the market is too small or won't pay," that is the real secret, and it is held by the incumbents' corpses, not by the founders. Related: there is no "why now." No regulatory change, no new behavior, no technology shift is named that makes 2026 different from the years in which three attempts went nowhere. Without a secret or a timing argument, this is an incremental improvement (a cleaner feed, a one-tap RSVP) — the engineering question fails too.

## The questions I would ask the founder

1. Why exactly did Kuadr Events, Enter Event and Go Wild stall — ran out of money, couldn't get organizers to pay, couldn't get supply, or got outcompeted by Instagram? This is the single most important unknown in the brief. Open question for **/founder-competitors**.
2. What does a small informal organizer in Tirana actually have to spend? What is total money flowing through the wedge segment per month — ticket revenue, class fees, anything commissionable? If the honest answer is "close to zero," the wedge is a lead magnet, not a market. Open question for **/founder-pricing** (willingness to pay) and **/founder-cfo** (whether that revenue base can ever cover one full-time salary).
3. When Stage 1 hits a few thousand followers, what — concretely — prevents a Tirana-resident operator from cloning the feed in a week? What asset exists at that moment that they cannot copy?
4. Which founder goes full-time, on what savings or funding, and how many months of runway? A winner-take-all local race run part-time from London and Barcelona is a race entered to lose. Open question for **/founder-cfo**.
5. Will the attendees the wedge serves (hikers, book-club members) convert into the audience commercial organizers will later pay to reach, or are they a different crowd? Open question for **/founder-consumer**.
6. Re-derive the 100–150 paying clients break-even figure once Stage 3 prices exist — and state how many *wedge-sized* organizers that implies, since they pay less than commercial ones. Open question for **/founder-cfo**.

## If FUND IF: the conditions

Not applicable — I vote PASS. For the record, what would move me to FUND IF: a convincing competitor autopsy showing the three local platforms failed for fixable reasons (execution, not market size); evidence of a real profit pool inside the wedge, not just attention; a named full-time founder resident in Tirana with stated runway from day one of Stage 1, not after it; and an articulated secret — something these founders know about this market that the incumbents and Instagram do not. The staged plan limits the cost of being wrong, which is admirable, but the Monopoly lens scores the destination, not the cheapness of the trip — and as briefed, the destination has no moat until long after the moment of maximum copyability.
